# Writing patterns

Use these patterns as editing signals. Except for the em-dash rule, do not replace a word or structure merely because it appears here. Consider its purpose, frequency, and the surrounding voice.

## State the point

Remove staged openings such as "let's dive in", "here's the thing", "honestly?", and "what really matters" when they only delay the point.

Remove unsupported contrasts such as "it is not X, it is Y" when nobody claimed X. Keep a contrast when both sides carry information or it corrects a real misunderstanding.

Remove fake objections and discarded options that no reader would seriously consider. Keep genuine alternatives in arguments, design documents, and instructions.

Cut one-line closers that repeat the previous paragraph, forced punchlines, generic lessons, and rows of dramatic fragments. A short sentence is fine when it adds information.

Replace decorative sayings and vague profundity with the specific claim. Watch for phrases such as "at its core", "the heart of the matter", "the language of", and "a testament to".

## Fix mechanical rhythm

Do not force ideas into groups of three. Keep three items when there are genuinely three distinct items.

Vary repeated sentence openings only when the repetition is accidental. Preserve deliberate repetition used for rhythm or emphasis.

Remove stacked qualifiers such as "could potentially possibly" while keeping real uncertainty, scope limits, legal qualifications, and safety warnings.

Use active voice when naming the actor improves clarity. Passive voice is valid when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately de-emphasised.

Never use an em dash. Rewrite the relationship between clauses using a comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence.

## Remove inflation

Prefer plain verbs such as "is", "has", and "uses" over "serves as", "boasts", "showcases", and similar inflation when the longer phrase adds nothing.

Remove exaggerated significance, generic optimism, and vague claims about impact or legacy. End on the last useful fact instead of "the future looks bright".

Treat stock words as signals when several cluster together. Common examples include crucial, delve, enduring, enhance, intricate, landscape, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, and vibrant. Keep any word that is accurate and natural in context.

Remove sales language from neutral prose. Watch for "breathtaking", "must-visit", "renowned", "nestled", "groundbreaking", and "in the heart of".

Do not use unnamed experts, observers, reports, media lists, or follower counts to manufacture authority. Preserve a source when the text identifies who said what.

Replace vague relationships such as "associated with" only when the source states the exact relationship. Never invent a role.

Remove shallow participial riders such as "highlighting", "underscoring", "showcasing", and "symbolising" when they add an interpretation unsupported by the source.

## Remove template residue

Delete chatbot greetings, praise, offers, and sign-offs when the text must stand alone. Examples include "Great question", "I hope this helps", and "Would you like me to continue?"

Remove knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and plausible guesses. State only what the available source supports.

Remove headings that repeat the first sentence, decorative emoji headings, unnecessary horizontal rules, and bold labels that add no structure.

Use sentence case for headings unless the writer or publication follows another style.

Describe the current subject rather than narrating how the document was drafted or what an earlier version said, except in changelogs, release notes, and migration guides.

## Preserve human choices

Keep unusual concrete details, mixed feelings, uncertainty, humour, asides, self-corrections, cultural references, and uneven rhythm when they belong to the writer.

Do not treat perfect grammar, formal vocabulary, curly quotation marks, one short sentence, a real disclaimer, or a real alternative as proof of artificial writing.

Do not rewrite text inside quotations, titles, proper names, code, commands, paths, URLs, or examples that discuss the watched phrase itself.
