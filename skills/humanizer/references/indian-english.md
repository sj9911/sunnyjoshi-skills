# Indian English and language mixing

Use this reference when the writer, audience, or subject is Indian, or when the user requests Indian English, Hinglish, or another Indian language mix.

India does not have one universal voice. Infer the register from the source and context. Never manufacture an accent, regional identity, class background, religion, or age.

## Neutral Indian English

Use natural, internationally readable Indian English unless the source or user asks for something more local.

- Follow the writer's spelling. Without a sample or established preference, Indian and British spellings such as colour, organise, travelling, and programme are acceptable.
- Preserve established Indian English words when they suit the audience. Examples include prepone, batchmate, lakh, crore, and out of station.
- Do not add phrases such as "do the needful", "kindly revert", honorifics, or local slang merely to make the writing sound Indian.
- Preserve names, place names, institutions, foods, festivals, kinship terms, and regional words exactly unless the user requests transliteration or translation.
- Keep the original level of politeness. Do not make professional Indian writing artificially casual or excessively deferential.

## Numbers, money, dates, and time

- For Indian money and India-focused quantities, prefer the source's lakh/crore system and Indian digit grouping. Do not convert between lakh/crore and million/billion unless asked.
- Preserve the source currency. Use INR or the rupee symbol when the source refers to Indian rupees.
- Avoid ambiguous numeric dates. When free to restyle, use forms such as "15 March 2026".
- Preserve IST and 12-hour or 24-hour time according to the source and channel.
- Do not change measurements or monetary values while rewriting.

## Channel and register

### Professional email

Be clear and courteous. Put the request or decision early. Retain necessary salutations and sign-offs. Replace vague formalities only when the intended action is already present in the source.

### WhatsApp and chat

Use shorter sentences and the source's natural contractions, punctuation, and line breaks. Do not add slang, emojis, or language mixing that the writer did not use.

### LinkedIn and public posts

Remove inflated inspiration, forced lessons, fake vulnerability, excessive hashtags, and generic celebration. Keep the writer's real experience and point of view.

### Articles, academic prose, and documentation

Keep domain terminology, citations, qualifications, and formal structure. Indianisation should affect locale and natural phrasing, not academic rigour or technical accuracy.

## Hinglish and other language mixes

Hinglish is opt-in. Do not assume that an Indian user speaks Hindi.

- Use Hinglish only when requested or already present in the source.
- Match the source's English-to-Hindi balance instead of increasing it.
- Preserve Devanagari or Romanised Hindi as supplied. Do not switch scripts unless asked.
- Do not invent Hindi vocabulary to make English text seem local. Use words supplied by the writer or common words whose meaning is unambiguous in context.
- Preserve the level of address and politeness already present. Do not add bhai, yaar, ji, sir, ma'am, or similar forms without evidence from the source.
- Apply the same rules to Tamil-English, Bengali-English, Malayalam-English, Marathi-English, and other combinations. Follow the writer's language mix rather than treating Hinglish as the national default.

## Final check

Confirm that the rewrite sounds natural to the intended Indian reader without exaggerating Indian identity. Check that no regional cue, cultural detail, language switch, or social relationship was invented.
